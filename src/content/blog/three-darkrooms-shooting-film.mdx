I describe myself as **full-stack, half-painter** — and the third thing, the one that ties the two together, is photography. I still shoot **35mm film** in 2026, on purpose, and I built this site to be the darkroom I develop it in. This post is about both halves: why analog still earns its place when my phone takes technically better pictures, and how a developer publishes photographs without leaking the things a photograph quietly carries.

If you came here for the code, skip to ["The digital darkroom"](#the-digital-darkroom). If you came for the film, start at the top.

## Why film, when the phone is objectively better

The phone wins every measurable contest — resolution, low light, dynamic range, and it's always in my pocket. Film wins the ones you can't measure.

- **A roll has 36 frames.** Scarcity changes how you see. When every press of the shutter costs something and can't be reviewed, you slow down, you wait, you don't spray. The keeper rate goes up because the throwaway rate goes down before you ever release the shutter.
- **You commit at capture, not in edit.** There's no chimping, no "I'll fix it later." The picture is decided when you take it. That constraint is the point.
- **The look isn't a filter.** Grain, halation, the way a stock like Portra renders skin and shadow — it's chemistry, not a slider. You can approximate it; you can't quite fake the discipline that produced the frame.

None of this makes film "better." It makes it a **different practice**, and the practice is what I'm after. The Bosphorus at 07:11, an empty U-Bahn platform at 23:54, horses at Tempelhof on Portra 400 — those are frames I got because I was shooting film and paying attention, not because the gear was good.

## The digital darkroom

Here's where the developer half takes over. Publishing photographs online is easy; publishing them **responsibly** is the interesting engineering problem, because a photo file carries more than the picture.

Every camera writes **EXIF metadata** into the file: GPS coordinates, the exact timestamp, camera serial numbers, sometimes software fingerprints. Post a JPEG straight from the camera and you've published where you stood, when, and with what. So the pipeline does two things at once:

1. **Strip metadata on delivery.** Every image served on the site has its metadata block stripped — GPS, serials, timestamps, everything. The file a visitor can download is naked.
2. **Keep the caption rich anyway.** The camera, lens, aperture, and shutter you read under each photo aren't pulled from the delivered file — they're fetched once from the original at build time and baked into the page. Best of both: the story stays, the coordinates don't.

On top of that, each image gets a **watermark** and is capped at a web-friendly size. It's not DRM — anyone determined can screenshot anything — but a visible signature plus a size ceiling is the honest amount of protection a portfolio actually warrants. The rest is theater, and I'd rather not pretend otherwise.

## Why the whole site looks like a darkroom

The aesthetic isn't decoration for its own sake. The site is built around a **terminal-and-darkroom** metaphor — phosphor text, a safelight red, a contact sheet on the homepage, photographs organized as *rolls* you page through frame by frame. It's the through-line between the three things I do: **code in a terminal, develop in a darkroom, paint in a studio** — three darkrooms, one place.

That framing also made the product decisions easier. A contact sheet wants to be browsed, not clicked-through. A roll wants prev/next that rolls smoothly, like advancing film, not a page reload between every frame. When the metaphor is doing real work, the interface mostly designs itself.

## What analog taught the developer

The two halves feed each other more than I expected.

Film taught me **constraint as a feature** — a roll of 36, a fixed ISO, no undo — and that's exactly the instinct behind shipping a site with almost no JavaScript and no cookies. Fewer moving parts, chosen deliberately, tend to produce better work than infinite options used carelessly.

And building the pipeline taught me to respect what a photograph carries. A picture is never just the picture. It's a place and a time and, if you're careless, a set of coordinates. Handling that well — stripping what shouldn't travel, keeping what tells the story — is the same care I try to bring to the frame itself.
