I keep this one bookmarked because it names something I feel every working day: **the better AI gets at writing code, the more the domain judgment becomes the job.** I lean on AI assistance heavily — most of my recent projects were built with an AI pair at the keyboard — and the article's core claim matches my experience exactly. The model accelerates the typing. It does not supply the judgment about *what is worth typing*.

That judgment is almost always domain-shaped. When I build for the German market, the decisive calls aren't syntax — they're knowing that you can't load Google Fonts from Google's CDN without a consent problem, that a contact form needs a lawful basis and an honest privacy policy, that "just add analytics" quietly becomes a cookie-banner question. On a musician's site the win wasn't the framework; it was knowing that events need the right structured data to show up in search and AI answers. On a translation office's site it was understanding the actual workflow before automating any of it. An AI will happily write all of that code — but only once *someone* has decided it's the right code. That someone is still you.

The part I'd especially point you to is the framing of expertise as **the ability to ask the right question and recognize a wrong answer.** That's the muscle AI doesn't replace and, if anything, makes more valuable: when a tool can produce a confident, plausible, wrong solution in seconds, the person who can smell that it's wrong is worth more, not less. Across a stack that ranges from Astro and Svelte front-ends to Supabase and Cloudflare back-ends to scraping and lead-gen pipelines, the thing that transfers between all of them isn't a language — it's that judgment.

It provoked a small reframe for me: I've stopped thinking of AI as a junior developer I delegate to, and started treating it as a very fast, very well-read collaborator who has never actually shipped anything to my users. It knows everything and is accountable for nothing. The accountability — the domain, the users, the consequences — stays with me. That's not a limitation of the tools; it's the part of the work that was always the point. Worth a read if you're figuring out where you add value in an AI-assisted workflow.
